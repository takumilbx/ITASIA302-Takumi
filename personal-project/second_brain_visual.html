01  ·  PKM

What is PKM?

Personal knowledge management, or PKM, is the set of steps a person uses to collect, organize, store, find, and share what they know. In simple terms, it means having a real system for your own knowledge instead of just hoping you will remember things later. The key idea is that the individual, not a company or an institution, is the person in charge of managing it.

The name is new, but the habit is very old. The term PKM comes from a 1999 working paper by Frand and Hixon (1999), which is fairly recent. The practice itself goes back centuries, from Leonardo da Vinci's notebooks to Niklas Luhmann's Zettelkasten, a box of more than 90,000 linked index cards. Even the idea of a machine for it is old. In 1945, Vannevar Bush imagined the Memex, a desk that could store everything you read and let you find it again quickly (Bush, 1945).

So why does such an old habit suddenly need its own research field? The reason is that the amount of information we deal with has grown very fast. Once Peter Drucker (1968) described the modern "knowledge worker", whose main asset is what they know, managing that knowledge stopped being optional. A simple way to picture PKM is the difference between a library and a pile. Both of them hold all your books, but only one of them will give you the right book before the deadline.

02  ·  Second Brain

And a second brain?

If PKM is the general idea, the second brain is its most famous version. A second brain is an external, digital place where you keep the ideas and information you collect through learning and experience. But storage by itself is not the point. Tiago Forte describes it as a system that "expands our memory and our intellect using the modern tools of technology and networks." What makes it more than simple note-taking is that it should also help you find, compare, shorten, and reuse your notes. A folder full of saved articles is just a digital attic. A second brain is meant to be a thinking partner.

The idea is centuries old, but the name is recent. Forte introduced his "Building a Second Brain" method in 2017 and explained it fully in his 2022 book of the same name (Forte, 2022). He gave the practice two simple structures. CODE stands for Capture, Organize, Distill, and Express. PARA stands for Projects, Areas, Resources, and Archives, and it sorts notes by how useful they are for action rather than by topic. So PKM is the wider category, and the second brain is one specific version of it. If PKM is cooking, the second brain is one particular and very well-marketed recipe.

In their 1998 paper "The Extended Mind", the philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers argued that thinking does not only happen inside the head. If Otto trusts his notebook and uses it all the time, it becomes a real part of his mind. Moving information into a system you trust is not laziness — it is cognitive extension.
Clark & Chalmers, "The Extended Mind" (1998)
03  ·  The Hype

Everyone is selling a brain

Spend any time in the productivity side of the internet and someone will try to sell you a brain. Forte's book was a bestseller. A whole industry of online creators now shows off note-taking "systems" with the kind of energy that people usually save for skincare routines. The newest promise is that AI will finally make the whole thing effortless. One 2025 academic paper describes this path clearly, moving from PKM, to the second brain, to a "personal AI companion" (Aal and Rüller, 2025). The market is huge and the discussion never stops. Whether any of it actually works is treated as a separate and slightly awkward question.

Two Persistent Failure Modes

  • 1. The Productivity Trap The system needs so much care that you spend more time looking after it than actually using it.
  • 2. The Collecting Habit Saving articles that you will never open again, until the second brain becomes a digital attic.

There is also a deeper problem. There is very little solid research showing that these systems really improve thinking. Most of the evidence is just personal stories. Some scholars even doubt the basic idea. William Jones (2007) argued that you can only really manage information, because knowledge is personal and lives in the mind, so it cannot be tidied up from the outside in the same way. In short, the field gives a lot of confident advice based on surprisingly little proof. That gap between loud claims and quiet evidence is exactly where this project starts, and it is also what the next beat is about.

04  ·  The Meme
Required part: Motivation

So why bother?

The sharpest critics of this whole genre enjoy pointing out something funny. The people who promote "second brains" often still cannot remember where their own car keys are. The partner who quietly keeps track of the keys, the glasses, and the kid's appointments was the original second brain all along.

Based on the "second brain" meme by @nunoei, paraphrased

Motivation. I did not come to this topic through theory. I came to it through failure. For years I saved notes and told myself that organizing them was a job for Future Me, who would surely have more time and more discipline. Future Me never showed up. My vault slowly turned into a beautifully tagged graveyard. The industry's answer is always the same one, which is the next template, the next app, or the next creator's setup. I bought in again and again. The meme above deserves a real answer, and so does the question hiding under it. Is the failure mine, or is it the method's? That question is the whole reason for this project.

05  ·  What I'm Building
Required parts: Project idea · Format

A second brain that actually helps me think

Project idea. I am not building a fancier filing cabinet. I am building a system where small, single-idea notes connect into arguments, where research builds on itself instead of just piling up, and where the question "do I really understand this?" has a clear, structural answer. The main idea behind it is simple. Most PKM advice focuses on capture, which means getting everything in. But the thing that really decides whether a system works is retrieval, which means getting the right idea back out at the moment you are thinking. So the project builds a second brain that puts retrieval first, and then tests that idea honestly.

Format. The final product makes this case as a digital essay on the web. It is a small set of linked pages rather than one long document. It includes this overview, a visual companion that you scroll through, a full written essay, and a section with data. The format is part of the argument. A project about how to organize knowledge should be easy to move around in. A flat PDF would quietly work against its own point.

06  ·  Current State
Required parts: Sources · Methods

Where I am now

Sources · Methods. The system is a pipeline. A paper enters through Zotero. It becomes a structured literature note in Obsidian. Claude Code runs a NotebookLM MCP integration to extract candidate atoms from the PDF. Verified atoms land in the vault, cluster into molecules, and synthesize into alloys that get tracked in Notion as actionable work. Each step below is animated. Step 03 · Claude Code + NLM MCP

Extract atoms via NotebookLM

Claude Code reads the literature note, opens the PDF via the Zotero path, and runs the NotebookLM MCP integration. Three focused queries return candidate atoms. Each suggestion gets verified against the source. Verified atoms land in /Atoms/, rejected ones get logged with reasons.

Verified atoms land in the vault

Each verified suggestion becomes its own file in /Atoms/. Filename is the claim itself as a full declarative sentence, findable by search alone. The vault currently holds 1,056 of them across thesis topics: TPACK, PKM, Japan's GIGA program, Bangkok's BMA pilot, AI epistemics.

Step 05 · Molecules

Atoms cluster into molecules

When 2 to 5 atoms share a theme, they get pulled into a molecule: a connection note with no new claims, only relations. The molecule lives in /Molecules/ and links back to its atoms. This is where pattern recognition stops being implicit and becomes a saved object.

Step 06 · Alloys

Molecules fuse into alloys

An alloy is a thesis-grade synthesis: an original argument built from molecules and atoms. It can never be drafted directly from a source. It needs molecules underneath it. Alloys are where this project's actual research output lives, in /Alloys/, each one tagged to a chapter of the thesis.

Step 07 · Notion

Alloys surface in Notion

Notion is where the system meets actual work. Each alloy gets surfaced as a thesis chapter draft, a fieldwork milestone, or an unfinished argument needing one more source. Deadlines, status pills, and writing tasks reference back to vault notes by citekey. Obsidian is the brain. Notion is the calendar.
