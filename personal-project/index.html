Building a Second Brain (For Real This Time)

Obsidian, Zotero, Notion, MCP connections, AI agents, and an honest attempt to build a system that actually makes me think better.

Takumi  ·  ITASIA302, University of Tokyo  ·  May 2026
00  ·  Introduction

What this is

The "second brain" is everywhere right now. Tiago Forte's Building a Second Brain became a productivity bestseller. Andrej Karpathy tweeted about his personal knowledge systems. An entire genre of tech influencers promises that agentic AI will finally let you capture and retrieve everything you've ever thought, your notes, your papers, your half-finished ideas, surfaced at exactly the right moment, forever. The market is enormous. The discourse is relentless. Whether any of it actually works is a separate question.

I was genuinely curious. Not because I'm already productive and want a fancier filing cabinet, but because I wasn't sure whether a PKM system would help me understand things I couldn't understand before, or just give me a more sophisticated way to avoid doing the work. The starting point was Zettelkasten, the slip-box method Niklas Luhmann used to write over 70 books. The idea that knowledge compounds when atomic notes link to each other rather than pile up in folders felt like the right structure to build on. So I built a system around it: Obsidian as the core vault with a Zettelkasten-style Atoms-Molecules-Alloys hierarchy, Zotero for reference management, Notion for project tracking, MCP connections to wire tools together, and AI agents to handle retrieval and synthesis. The four data visualizations documented elsewhere on this site are one part of that workflow: how I processed raw datasets into something navigable before the insights moved into the vault. This site is the Express step, turning accumulated knowledge into an argument about what the system is, how it was built, and what it actually produced.

Before getting into the framework, though, it's worth naming what the hype tends to obscure. Because the second brain industry sells individual productivity solutions to what is often a structural and relational problem. Someone on the internet said it better than any academic paper I read.

"Excuse me while I laugh at tech bros hyping 'second brains' who still have to ask their wife where the car keys are, where the glasses are, when the kid's next doctor's appointment is... Your wife's brain IS your second brain, the one you've been using for free all along, offloading your mental clutter onto her with unlimited tokens."
— @nunoei: "Before you have a second brain, you need to have a first brain first"

The joke lands in two directions. One: PKM hype is often rebranded executive dysfunction with a Notion subscription. Two, and more seriously: cognitive labor is already being offloaded and distributed all the time, just onto people rather than tools, and without credit or compensation. The wife was doing RAG before RAG was cool. With that on the table, here is what the three sources actually contribute, and here is what I did with them.

01  ·  The historical anchor

The second brain is five hundred years old

Every claim in the second brain hype cycle has a date attached, and the date is much older than the discourse admits. In 1545 the Swiss naturalist Conrad Gessner set out to catalog every book in existence and warned his readers about the "confusing and harmful abundance of books." Ann Blair's history of early modern information management shows that the feeling my generation attributes to the internet, the sense of drowning in more material than one life can process, was already a standard scholarly complaint before the printing press was a century old. That reframes the whole project. If the overload is nearly five hundred years old, the interesting question is not how to finally capture everything. It is what each generation's capture technology did to the people who used it.

Read in that light, my Obsidian vault stops looking like a product of the 2020s and starts looking like the newest layer of a very long sediment. The commonplace book becomes the card index, which Markus Krajewski calls a "paper machine" and reads as a discrete processing machine three centuries before the computer. The card index becomes Vannevar Bush's imagined memex, the memex becomes hypertext, and hypertext becomes the bidirectional link I now take for granted. Niklas Luhmann sits in the middle of that chain, not at its origin. The timeline below walks through five stations. Each one names the problem it solved and the part of it that survives, unchanged, inside my own system.

What it solved
What survives in my vault

Three things stay constant across all five stations: notes are kept small, notes are given addresses, and notes are connected to other notes. Atomicity, addressing, linking. What changes at each station is who performs the synthesis, the step where separate notes become a new thought. For four hundred and eighty years the answer was always the same: the human does, and the tool only holds the pieces still long enough for the human to see the pattern. The fifth station is the first one where that answer wavers, because a language model can now draft the synthesis itself. That is the genuinely new thing, and it is exactly the step I refused to automate. The pipeline in the next section delegates capture, extraction, and retrieval to machines, but every atom passes through manual verification, and every alloy is written by me. Not because the model cannot draft it, but because the four previous stations suggest that the drafting is the thinking.

02  ·  The workflow

How the system actually works

The system is a pipeline. A paper enters through Zotero. It becomes a structured literature note in Obsidian. Claude Code runs a NotebookLM MCP integration to extract candidate atoms from the PDF. Verified atoms land in the vault, cluster into molecules, and synthesize into alloys that get tracked in Notion as actionable work. Each step below is animated. Each animation uses a real artifact from the vault.

Step 01 · Zotero

Capture the paper

Every source enters the system through Zotero. PDF attached, metadata extracted, citekey auto-generated using the authorYEAR convention. The Obsidian Zotero Integration plugin then drops a structured import note into the vault inbox, ready for processing.

Step 03 · Claude Code + NLM MCP

Extract atoms via NotebookLM

Claude Code reads the literature note, opens the PDF via the Zotero path, and runs the NotebookLM MCP integration. Three focused queries return candidate atoms. Each suggestion gets verified against the source. Verified atoms land in /Atoms/, rejected ones get logged with reasons.

Step 04 · Atoms

Verified atoms land in the vault

Each verified suggestion becomes its own file in /Atoms/. Filename is the claim itself as a full declarative sentence, findable by search alone. The vault currently holds 1,056 of them across thesis topics: TPACK, PKM, Japan's GIGA program, Bangkok's BMA pilot, AI epistemics.

Step 05 · Molecules

Atoms cluster into molecules

When 2 to 5 atoms share a theme, they get pulled into a molecule: a connection note with no new claims, only relations. The molecule lives in /Molecules/ and links back to its atoms. This is where pattern recognition stops being implicit and becomes a saved object.

Step 06 · Alloys

Molecules fuse into alloys

An alloy is a thesis-grade synthesis: an original argument built from molecules and atoms. It can never be drafted directly from a source. It needs molecules underneath it. Alloys are where this project's actual research output lives, in /Alloys/, each one tagged to a chapter of the thesis.

Step 07 · Notion

Alloys surface in Notion

Notion is where the system meets actual work. Each alloy gets surfaced as a thesis chapter draft, a fieldwork milestone, or an unfinished argument needing one more source. Deadlines, status pills, and writing tasks reference back to vault notes by citekey. Obsidian is the brain. Notion is the calendar.

03  ·  What the system produced

What the system produced

Four concrete outputs from the workflow above. Each one started as a dataset, got compressed into navigable patterns through visualization, and then moved into the vault as atoms. They are the Distill step in Forte's CODE workflow made visible. Each gets a description, a link, and an honest account of what it taught me.

01a  ·  Japanese EdTech Research Trends Live
JMET / JJET research trends, 1977–2025

4,280 articles from JMET and JJET (1977–2025), classified into 42 themes across 7 groups. Six interactive panels: trend lines, inflection points, co-occurrence heatmap, UpSet chart, territory map, and methodology. It took a while.

What I learned

The GIGA School program rollout in 2021 was a watershed moment. It shifted research attention from physical hardware and infrastructural connectivity (which dominated the 1980s and 1990s) to pedagogical integration and teacher professional development (specifically measured via the TPACK framework). Simply put, the national policy solved the device access problem, but it created an immediate pedagogical bottleneck at the classroom level.

01b  ·  AI & Society Research Trends Live
How a field's conversation about AI changed

1,849 articles from the journal AI & Society (2021–2025). A scrolling trace of how a field's conversation about AI evolved over five years, including the very obvious moment when ChatGPT dropped.

What I learned

The longitudinal shift in paper topics from 2021 to 2025 reveals a field undergoing a rapid cognitive transition. Physical embodiment topics (such as Robots and HRI) declined steeply from 18.9% to 3.7%, whereas cognitive and linguistic automation (specifically Generative AI and LLMs) surged from 0% to 24% after 2023. Meanwhile, discussions of Ethics and Governance remained stable at approximately 15%, indicating that ethical concern did not shift, only the technologies being scrutinized.

04  ·  The framework

The framework, and its failure modes

The structure I'm using to make sense of these four datasets comes from three sources on personal knowledge management. But before getting into what they argue, it's worth naming the ways this whole genre tends to go wrong. Because if you've spent any time in PKM spaces online, you've probably seen more elaborate Obsidian dashboards than finished essays.

The failure modes
  • The optimization trap

    Building the system becomes the task. Rearranging notes instead of writing. Picking tools instead of using them. The second brain becomes a way to feel productive without producing anything. Anyone who has spent a Sunday afternoon perfecting their weekly review template instead of doing the actual weekly review knows exactly what I mean.

  • Analysis paralysis

    More data, more frameworks, more structure, but no clearer sense of what to do next. Over-capture leads to overwhelm. The inbox becomes a graveyard of things you meant to think about.

  • The first brain problem

    A PKM system amplifies your thinking, it doesn't replace it. If you don't have a clear research question, no amount of linked notes will give you one. You'll just have very well-organized confusion.

With those caveats in place, here's what the three sources contribute:

@FORTE2022
Building a Second Brain (2022), Tiago Forte
Core contribution

CODE workflow (Capture, Organize, Distill, Express) and PARA system (Projects, Areas, Resources, Archives). A second brain externalizes thinking so attention can focus on synthesis, not storage. The promise is that you stop re-reading the same paper for the fourth time because you actually wrote down what it said.

Applied here

The vault is the Organize layer. PARA folders structure everything by actionability, not subject. Zotero handles the Capture pipeline: papers arrive via automatic import and land in the inbox before processing. The visualization work sits in Distill: each dataset compressed into atoms before the insights moved into the vault. This website is the Express step, turning accumulated atoms, molecules, and alloys into a coherent argument about how the system was built and what it actually produced.

@JI2011
Knowledge Base Population: Successful Approaches and Challenges (2011), Ji & Grishman
Core contribution

Ji and Grishman analyzed 23 competing systems in the 2010 Knowledge Base Population shared task: the computational problem of building a structured knowledge base from raw text at scale. Their central finding is that the hard problem is not storage but cross-document entity resolution and retrieval. A naive web-popularity baseline outperformed 24 of 46 system runs at 71% accuracy, meaning intuitive salience filters can beat elaborate analytical ones. Combining multiple independent extraction pipelines through ML re-ranking yielded up to 4.7% absolute improvement over any single system.

Applied here

The computational framing makes explicit what PKM systems are actually trying to solve technically. The entity linking problem, resolving which notes refer to the same concept across thousands of documents, is exactly what Obsidian's wikilink graph addresses. The salience finding maps onto Forte's resonance filter: trusting felt relevance over exhaustive checklists is not sloppy, it reflects how effective knowledge retrieval actually works. The system-combination finding is why this project uses multiple tools rather than one: Obsidian, Zotero, Notion, and MCP connections each handle a different part of the pipeline.

@FERREIRA2026
How People Manage Knowledge in Their "Second Brains" (2026), Ferreira et al.
Core contribution

The only empirical study of how knowledge workers actually use Obsidian in practice. Seven Computer Science researchers at a Brazilian industry lab, studied through semi-structured interviews and live observation of their workflows. Key finding: planned retrieval strategies fundamentally determine how people organize their vaults from the outset. Organization encodes anticipated search intent. Three temporal phases observed: capture on the fly, structural formatting, then future retrieval. Pain point identified: starting from a blank structure is overwhelming, and scaffolded templates significantly reduce onboarding friction.

Applied here

The vault in this project was built retrieval-first. Atom filenames are full declarative sentences, findable without surrounding context. The template system exists precisely because the blank-page pain point Ferreira et al. identified is real: hitting it on day one is what made a structured folder hierarchy and CLAUDE.md feel necessary rather than over-engineered. Their proposed AI features, linking organizational templates to anticipated retrieval strategies, describe what the MCP connections and AI agents in this project attempt to do in practice.

A visualization is an intermediate packet. It externalizes pattern-finding so your attention can move to interpretation. But it only works if you actually stop and interpret it, rather than immediately building the next one. I say this as someone who built four.

05  ·  Synthesis

Cross-visualization synthesis

What the four datasets say when they're in the same room together.

When placed in the same room, these four datasets reveal a persistent tension between top-down digital infrastructure mandates and bottom-up pedagogical adaptation. In the Japanese context, the rapid device rollout of the GIGA School program solved the physical access problem, yet it immediately created an instructional bottleneck: a digital tool is only as effective as the pedagogy supporting it. This friction is compounded by a profound temporal mismatch; commercial Generative AI applications iterate and deploy in weekly cycles, whereas teacher professional development and curriculum redesign operate on multi-year institutional timelines.

Similarly, the civic data from the Bangkok Metropolitan Administration highlights that challenges in transit, environment, and education do not exist in administrative isolation; they co-occur as coupled, systemic dependencies. When educational technology policies are implemented without acknowledging these coupled municipal realities, they risk failing due to external systemic pressures. An effective research synthesis must therefore examine how national technology frameworks adapt to local classroom constraints, recognizing that systemic coordination is required to bridge the gap between policy design and lived educational experience.

06  ·  Thesis direction

Where this is pointing

The concrete output of this whole process. A candidate thesis direction, with a brief account of how the data led me there.

Based on the patterns identified across these visualizations, my research project is titled: "Bridging the Pedagogical Gap: A Qualitative Comparative Study of Teacher TPACK Development in 1:1 Classrooms across Japan and Thailand." This study will investigate how classroom teachers navigate the transition to 1:1 device environments under contrasting national digital mandates. Specifically, the inquiry focuses on the contrast between the highly centralized top-down GIGA School initiative in Tokyo and the decentralized municipal school system in Bangkok.

The research design utilizes a qualitative comparative methodology. Through semi-structured interviews, classroom observations, and lesson plan analysis, I will examine how primary school teachers integrate technology into their subject-matter instruction. By applying the TPACK (Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge) framework as an analytical lens, the study aims to identify the specific institutional support systems, peer networks, and professional development models that successfully foster pedagogical adaptability.

Ultimately, this comparative approach aims to surface insights that single-country studies miss. By analyzing how teachers in Tokyo and Bangkok negotiate policy pressures and local classroom constraints, the study will provide actionable recommendations for educational administrators. The goal is to inform the design of teacher training programs that treat pedagogical development not as a standard technical training package, but as a situated, context-sensitive practice.

07  ·  Personal system design

What I'm actually going to do

The part that makes this project useful beyond the grade. A concrete design for how I'm going to manage knowledge, track work, and write research going forward. Not a generic PKM template lifted from a YouTube video. A system shaped by the failure modes I've actually experienced and what building these four visualizations revealed about how I actually work.

Design principles

My personal knowledge management system is built around three core design principles:

Retrieval-first naming: All notes and atomic packets must be named using full, declarative sentences rather than vague keywords. This ensures that ideas can be discovered via semantic search or link auto-completion without having to open and read the file first.

Temporal staging: Information is categorized by its active lifecycle stage rather than static topic classification. Notes must move through defined thresholds with clear exit criteria, preventing the build-up of unorganized digital clutter.

Tool-functional alignment: Each application is restricted to a single functional role in the pipeline. Rather than attempting to build an all-in-one workspace, tools are integrated based on their specific strengths, with clear interfaces separating capture, processing, synthesis, writing, and review.

The system
Stage Tool / Method Rule
Capture Zotero & Web Clipper All incoming sources must land in the Zotero inbox; no direct copy-pasting into the main workspace.
Process Obsidian Literature Notes Extract highlights, format metadata, and write initial impressions within 48 hours of capture.
Synthesize Obsidian Atoms & Alloys Deconstruct literature notes into atomic concept notes and build alloy outlines for synthesis.
Write Claude Code & Neovim Draft papers using Markdown, referencing citation keys directly from the Zotero bibliography.
Review Notion Project Boards Weekly review of active writing projects and source backlogs.
