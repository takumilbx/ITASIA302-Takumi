    ITASIA302 · Final Project

    Building a
    Second Brain

    ( actually functioning, not AI slop )

    The documentation of my journey wanting to learn better, remember better, and get everything in one place before I start writing my master's thesis.

    01 / 19 · The Problem

    One thing I really depend on is my memory

    Unlike a normal corporate job, or my bachelor's degree, master's studies rely heavily on me. I have to get everything together and organise it myself. It comes from everywhere. Meetings with my adviser, feedback, reading a single paper, insights from field observation. I cannot track all of it along the way.

    That is what happened in my first semester, and it really frustrated me. I know I had a really good idea, something that might benefit my thesis, and I could not remember it along the way. Then it becomes a loop. I have to read the paper again. I have to have the idea again.

    I am not really good at note taking either, so I keep forgetting things. I wanted to try something that helps me remember better, organise my thoughts, and pull out what I already have more easily.

    02 / 19 · Personal Knowledge Management

    What is PKM?

    I cannot remember things. Personal knowledge management is the thing I think could help me.

    For me it is a system that helps me get whatever information I want at the right time. It might not fit every person. Everyone has different learning styles and different ways of handling information, so a personal knowledge management system is one that fits your own style of managing knowledge. The point of this project is finding out what kind of system suits me, and then trying to build it.

    At first I was thinking the other way around. I thought it was a silver bullet that would organise my thoughts in a fancy way, something like Jarvis in the Iron Man movie. It is not about that. The word personal is the important one. The core of personal knowledge management is making it personal, there is no one size fits all solution, and you have to explore your way through it.

    • Collect, store, retrieve, and own the whole thing yourself. That last part is what separates it from a subscription.
    • The term goes back only to a 1998 EDUCOM paper by Frand and Hixson (Frand & Hixson, 1998). The habit is much older. Leonardo left more than 7,000 notebook pages mixing anatomy, engineering and art (Isaacson, 2017), and Luhmann's slip-box reached roughly 90,000 cards (Schmidt, 2016).
    • In 1945 Vannevar Bush described the Memex, a desk that stored everything you read and pulled it back along associative trails. The hard part he wrote about was the pulling back (Bush, 1945, sect. 6).
    • Drucker named knowledge work in 1959, and after that managing what you know stopped being optional (Drucker, 1959).

    03 / 19 · Second Brain

    And a second brain?

    • The famous version of the idea. Forte defines it as "a digital archive of your most valuable memories, ideas, and knowledge" (Forte, 2022, p. 4).
    • A folder of saved articles is an attic. The phrase thinking partner is doing a lot of work in that definition, and storage alone will not earn it.
    "For Otto, his notebook plays the role usually played by a biological memory." Clark & Chalmers, 1998, p. 12 · The Extended Mind

    Otto has memory loss and keeps his addresses in a notebook. He reaches for it the way you reach for a memory, so Clark and Chalmers argue the notebook has become part of his mind (Clark & Chalmers, 1998). That argument is what stopped me treating my own notes as a crutch.

    Field noteClark and Chalmers gave me permission to stop feeling guilty. I used to think that looking something up in my own notes was a kind of cheating, as though understanding meant holding it all in my skull. Otto's notebook counts as part of Otto's mind because he reaches for it without hesitating. I still hesitate before reaching for my notes. Less than I did in April.

    04 / 19 · Plain Language

    Six words, before the method

    Everything after this leans on six words. Two are apps, one is a folder, and three are sizes a note can be. I use them constantly and none of them are as technical as they sound.

    Vault

    A folder of plain text files on my laptop. That is the whole thing. No database, no account, no company. If every app I use shut down tomorrow, the notes would still open.

    Obsidian

    The app I read that folder with. It draws the links between notes and searches them quickly. It does not own the files, which is why I am willing to depend on it.

    Zotero

    Where the papers live. It keeps the PDF, the authors, the year and the citation together, so I have not typed a reference by hand in two years.

    Atom

    One note with one idea in it, named as a full sentence. If the name will not fit in a filename, it is two ideas and I have to split it.

    Molecule

    Two to five atoms that turned out to be about the same thing, linked into one note. It adds no claim of its own.

    Alloy

    An argument built out of molecules, chapter sized. No single source contains it, which is the point and also why it takes so long.

    Field noteTier one, tier two and tier three told me nothing about what belonged where. Atom, molecule and alloy at least tell you what size a thing is. A chemist would poke holes in it. I am not writing for chemists.

    05 / 19 · The Hype

    Everyone is selling a brain

    • Forte's book sold well, and there is now a creator economy demonstrating note-taking systems with the energy of a skincare routine.
    • The newest promise is that AI makes all of it effortless. There is already research mapping the path from PKM to second brain to a personal AI companion (Aal & Rüller, 2025).
    • Whether any of it makes you think better gets asked much less often. The movement is practitioner-driven, and the academic literature treats it as information management (Jones, 2010) or as speculative design (Aal & Rüller, 2025) rather than as anything measured.
    • Jones puts the deeper problem plainly. "Knowledge can be managed only indirectly, through the management of information" (Jones, 2010, Abstract). The knowing stays in a head.
    • I have hit two failure modes myself. In the Productivity Trap the upkeep eats the thinking. In the Collecting Habit the vault fills up with other people's ideas.
    Field noteI am not writing this from above the hype. I bought in. I watched the setup videos, downloaded the templates, and rebuilt my vault three times to match other people's screenshots. Every rebuild felt like progress and produced no finished writing. The thing the marketing never gets around to is proof, so that is where I started.

    • I got here through failure rather than theory. Organising was always a job for Future Me, and he never showed up.
    • The industry's answer is always another template.
    • A master's thesis does not fit in one head, so the stakes stopped being hypothetical.
    • Which leaves a question I cannot dodge. Is the failure mine, or the method's?
    Field noteMy vault had beautiful tags and dead notes. Nothing came back out. I laughed at the car keys meme and then recognised myself in it, which is a worse feeling than it sounds. So either I am undisciplined, which is what every new template quietly implies, or capture-first points the wrong way. A thesis is a fair place to test that, because it asks for the same idea back months after you filed it.

    07 / 19 · What I Am Building

    A brain that helps me think

    • Not a fancier filing cabinet. Small notes, one idea each, that connect into arguments.
    • Most advice obsesses over capture. Ferreira and colleagues found that the other end mattered more, and that how people plan to retrieve shapes how their knowledge bases get built and used (Ferreira et al., 2026, p. 72).
    • Memory research points the same way. Retrieval practice beats re-reading for long-term retention (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006).
    • So I built for retrieval first, then went looking for ways the claim might be wrong.
    Field noteRetrieval-first is a constraint rather than a slogan, and it decides small things. Filenames became full sentences, because I search in sentences. One idea per file, because two ideas in one file means one of them is gone. The test I apply is boring and specific. It is eleven at night, I am writing, and I need the paper I read in April.

    08 / 19 · The Stack

    The tools I use

    Five tools, in the order work moves through them. A paper goes in at one end and comes out as a note I can find.

    • Zotero · capture. Every source arrives with its PDF, its metadata and a citekey, and an import note lands in the vault inbox.
    • Obsidian · the vault. Plain text notes linked into a graph, reached by search and backlink rather than by folders.
    • Claude Code · the operator. It reads the literature note, opens the PDF, runs the extraction, and checks every candidate against the source.
    • NotebookLM · the extractor. Queries that can only answer from the source I hand it, which is what keeps the claims honest.
    • Notion · the calendar. Chapters, milestones and deadlines, linked back by citekey. The vault holds the thinking and Notion holds the dates.
    Field noteThis looks like tool clutter until you notice that each one holds a different kind of trust. Zotero holds provenance. Obsidian holds meaning. The AI layer I do not trust at all, which is why it sits where it does: everything it pulls out gets checked against the page it came from before it counts as an atom. On a normal run, two candidates in seven do not survive that check.

    10 / 19 · Zettelkasten

    The slip-box

    • Luhmann had worked with his slip-box for 26 years before he wrote about it (Luhmann, 1981, p. 223). Across its whole life, 1951 to 1997, it reached roughly 90,000 cards (Schmidt, 2016).
    • Every slip carries a fixed number that never changes, and the filing deliberately runs against topical order (Luhmann, 1981, pp. 223-224).
    • Each note "gains its quality from the network of references and back-references" (Luhmann, 1981, p. 225). Structure emerges rather than being imposed.
    • He set one condition for communication, that "the partners can surprise each other mutually" (Luhmann, 1981, pp. 222, 225). He meant the box could hold up its end.
    • Atom, molecule and alloy are my plain text rebuild of that, with one rule Luhmann never wrote down. One idea per note.
    Field noteThe ninety thousand cards are not what convinced me. Luhmann wrote about the box as something that could surprise him, and I had never thought about notes that way. Mine started doing it somewhere past the thousandth atom. I searched for something about teacher judgment and got back a note I had no memory of writing, sitting next to two I did. I still cannot tell you how to make that happen sooner.

    11 / 19 · The Method

    The framework I apply

    A Zettelkasten built around retrieval, where Forte's CODE and PARA are built around capture (Forte, 2022, pp. 42-49, 87-111). Knowledge climbs three tiers, and nothing moves up until the tier below holds.

    • Atom. One idea, one exact quote, one source. The filename is the claim written as a full sentence, so search alone can find it. The vault holds 2,163.
    • Molecule. Two to five atoms on a shared theme. No new claims, only the relations between the ones already there.
    • Alloy. An original synthesis at thesis grade. It can never be drafted straight from a source.
    Field noteForte's CODE moves material toward expression and PARA sorts it by how actionable it is. Both are built around getting things in. Mine is built around getting things out, which sounds like a small difference and is not. The rule that nothing moves up until the tier below holds is the first thing I would drop if I were being lazy, and it is the part doing the work.

    12 / 19 · Atom

    Capture one idea

    • One claim, in my words. The exact quote and the source sit underneath it.
    • If it fits a clean filename it is atomic. If it does not, it is two ideas sharing a coat.
    • The filename is the claim, so searching for the thought finds it without me remembering the book.
    atom · verified · live from the vault

    Friction is feedback that generates moral awareness and preserves human agency.

    "Friction isn't failure. Friction is feedback." Selsbæk-Reitz, 2026, p. 91
    Field noteAtomicity is the discipline I resisted longest. My old notes were summaries, five ideas braided together, none of them findable. Splitting still feels wasteful while I am doing it. The payoff arrives months later and it is real: one of those sentences turns out to be the piece an argument was missing, and I find it by typing roughly what it says. The atom on this screen carries a verified stamp because an earlier draft of this essay got its source wrong and the check caught it.

    13 / 19 · Molecule

    Link into a molecule

    • Two to five atoms on a shared theme, linked into one note.
    • It adds no new claims, only the relations.
    • Never filed under a folder I chose. The grouping emerges, the way Luhmann described a note gaining its quality from the network around it (Luhmann, 1981, p. 225).
    • Pattern recognition stops being a feeling and becomes a file I can open.
    Field noteA molecule is what I write when I catch myself thinking that three papers are secretly about the same thing. That thought used to evaporate by morning. Now it becomes a file with three links and a name I have to commit to. The rule about adding no new claims is there because I kept breaking it.

    14 / 19 · Alloy

    Fuse into an alloy

    • Molecules fuse into a synthesis at thesis grade that no single source could hand me.
    • The metaphor comes from metallurgy. Solute atoms of a different size strain the lattice, and the alloy ends up harder than either pure metal (Callister & Rethwisch, 2018, sect. 7.9).
    • The argument holds because unlike ideas were forced together.
    • This is the tier my thesis chapters come out of.
    Field noteI picked metallurgy over the usual gardening metaphor on purpose. Gardens grow if you water them. Alloys need heat, pressure, and two metals that do not want to mix. The arguments I trust most are the ones where a Japanese survey, a Bangkok classroom and a framework paper from 2006 disagree with each other and I have to sit with it for a week before anything comes out.

    15 / 19 · Case Study

    The method on a real thesis problem

    My thesis asks how teachers build TPACK, the knowledge that folds technology into real teaching (Mishra & Koehler, 2006). I am comparing Japan's GIGA School Program with pilot classrooms run by the Bangkok Metropolitan Administration. I sit in on lessons at what Spradley calls the passive participation level, and interview afterwards (Spradley, 1980, pp. 58-59). What follows are live notes from that work, copied out of the vault as they stand.

    atom · field

    Teacher A frames science and ICT as well matched because the subject centers on observation and recording.

    atom · field

    Teacher A can state criteria for choosing analog over digital, but ultimately grounds the preference in tacit sensory judgment.

    atom · field

    A teaching move the observer read as deliberate pedagogy, Teacher A describes as not consciously intended.

    atom · lit

    Expert teachers in one-to-one classrooms give clear reasons for when not to use ICT; novices barely can (Yagisawa & Horita, 2017, p. 87).

    atom · lit

    Japanese experts apply a "harmonious attitude" (調和的態度): switching between traditional and ICT-based instruction as a mature strategy (Yagisawa & Horita, 2017).

    atom · lit

    Novices and veterans alike treat classroom discipline as the prerequisite for ICT integration (Yagisawa & Horita, 2017).

    The molecule they form: selective non-use of ICT is an advanced and partly tacit form of TPACK. Teacher A enacts the discrimination without naming it. Yagisawa's veterans name it, then ground it in the same situational judgment. Same competence, different levels of articulacy. Spradley's warning applies, that "a large part of any culture consists of tacit knowledge" (Spradley, 1979/2016, p. 9), which would leave this competence invisible to surveys and to formal training design. The discipline finding narrows the claim. Part of knowing when not to use ICT is baseline craft that novices have as well, so only the situational discernment counts as advanced.

    The gap shows up in the literature as well. Observed lessons run 64.1 percent technological-pedagogical knowledge, while fully integrated TPCK appears in about 1 percent (Yagisawa & Horita, 2019, p. 36). Experience-linked differences in ICT awareness persist into the GIGA era (Sakakibara et al., 2025, pp. 51-52). The framework blurs in practice, packaged "much too big" and "too small" at once (Brantley-Dias & Ertmer, 2013, p. 104). Empirically only the technology domain separates cleanly (Archambault & Barnett, 2010, p. 1659), and "very little theoretical development of the model has occurred" (Graham, 2011, p. 1954). My molecule sits in that gap.

    alloy · forming

    Knowing when not to use ICT as the mark of integrated TPACK.A chapter-grade argument the system surfaced. No single source contains it.

    Field noteThis section is why the project exists. I watched Teacher A run a science lesson with the tablets closed, and my first note called it a limitation. The interview corrected me. She could name her criteria for going analog, and then said the real reason was that she could feel it and could not put it into words. Six atoms, one molecule and an alloy that is still forming later, my misreading is turning into a chapter. I would not have caught the mistake without the interview, and I would not have found the literature that agrees with her without the vault.

    16 / 19 · Current State

    Where I am now

    One source, moved by hand through seven steps. Zotero capture, Obsidian literature note, extraction checked against the source page, atoms, molecules, alloys, Notion. It goes in as a PDF and comes out as a dated chapter draft.

    • A typical run produces 7 candidates, of which 5 survive and 2 do not. I care more about the 2.
    • The atoms on screen behind this text are real, including four distilled from the book this project studies (Forte, 2022, pp. 54, 179, 180, 236).
    • Tonight the vault holds 3,066 linked notes and 9,121 links. The counts were computed on 2026-07-28 by the project's own parse script, over the live vault.
    Field noteThis is where I use AI and where I learned to fence it in. NotebookLM answers only from the paper I hand it. Claude proposes atoms, and accepting one means opening the source page. A pipeline with no rejection step is not a pipeline. Seven steps is slower than it sounds like it should be, and it is the reason every claim here has a page number behind it.

    17 / 19 · Working Thesis

    The vault, and its claim

    Retrieval-first organization explains why capture-first Personal Knowledge Management templates keep failing their users. My thesis is the test case.

    Three sources carry it. Forte (2022) is the book that made capture-first a movement, studied here rather than leaned on. Luhmann (1981) shows the retrieval-first idea is older than any of the apps. Ferreira and colleagues (2026) supply the field evidence, that "the strategy participants use to create and organize their knowledge is strongly related to their intended retrieval strategy" (p. 74). Templates fail when they encode somebody else's retrieval strategy.

    Field noteThe thesis sentence changed while I was writing this, and I would rather show that than hide it. My earlier wording claimed more than Ferreira's study supports, and the verification pass caught it. What I ended up with is narrower. It claims what the sources carry, and it names my own thesis as the thing being tested. Watching my own claim shrink was not a good feeling. It was the right outcome.
