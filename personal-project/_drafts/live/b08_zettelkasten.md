## [body3] body
I came upon this concept a while ago, long before Claude Code and the whole agentic AI thing. It is a system called Zettelkasten, and it is the earlier version of the second brain system I use now. The method goes back to the sociologist Niklas Luhmann, who had worked with his slip-box for 26 years before he wrote about it [[(Luhmann, 1981, p. 223)]]. Over its whole life it reached roughly 90,000 cards [[(Schmidt, 2016)]]. I took the names atom, molecule and alloy from a video explaining the method [[(Wanderloots, 2024)]].

## [body4] body
Zettelkasten divides concepts and facts into smaller chunks called atoms. One atom is one claim, or one fact, from one source. When I read literature, or when I have an idea, I note it as an atom.

## [body5] body
From there, I link the atoms that share a concept into a cluster of ideas called molecules. No new argument gets made at this stage. Luhmann's point was that a note "gains its quality from the network of references and back-references" [[(Luhmann, 1981, p. 225)]], so the linking is the work.

## [body6] body
Then I combine molecules into a bigger chunk called alloys, which is where a new argument comes from, something I have thought of myself. Luhmann set one condition for the box, that "the partners can surprise each other mutually" [[(Luhmann, 1981, pp. 222, 225)]]. That is the process I use to organise my thoughts and to keep track of my own thinking.
