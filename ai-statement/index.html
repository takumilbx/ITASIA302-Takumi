Section 03  ·  Disclosure & Method

How these pages were made

Takumi  ·  UTokyo  ·  Spring 2026

Every page on this site was produced with help from AI tools. This is the page where I say which ones, what they did, and what I still insist on doing myself. I wrote this because a good statement about AI should be specific enough to argue with not a badge, not a disclaimer, but a record of method.

03.1  ·  The principle

Disclosure should be specific, not performative

A lot of AI disclosure right now is ceremonial a paragraph at the bottom of a page that says AI was used in the creation of this work and then leaves you with no way to tell what that means. That sentence is compatible with almost anything: a spell-checker, a ghostwriter, a co-author who made every argument.

For a course on Digital Technology and Society, I think that is the wrong instinct. The question is not whether AI was used. It is what it was used for, where judgment stayed with me, and what someone reading my work should trust more or less as a result.

The goal is not to prove I did not use AI. The goal is to be accurate about how I did.

The rest of this page is the long-form version of that. I list the tools I used, what each one did on this site, and, for each assignment, a split between what I brought and what the model brought. I would rather be specific and wrong somewhere than vague and technically correct everywhere.

03.2  ·  The tools

Seven tools, each used for something different

Different tools are good at different things. Treating AI as one monolith flattens a real distinction between a research assistant, a grammar checker, and an image generator. Below is the actual kit, and the role each one played.

Claude
Anthropic  ·  Cowork & Claude Code

The main collaborator. I use Claude through Cowork (a desktop assistant) and Claude Code (a terminal agent) as a thinking partner and an implementer that can read, write, and run code in my workspace.

Used for  site build, structure, copy iteration
NotebookLM
Google

Research synthesis across many sources at once. I load the course's annotated bibliography and ask targeted questions without the model making up citations it can only answer from what is in the notebook.

Used for  Research Trends literature synthesis
Grammarly AI
Grammarly

A quiet, line-level editor. It does not rewrite my voice; it flags the places I have written a five-clause sentence on autopilot and quietly offers a shorter one.

Used for  prose cleanup, tone consistency
Google Nano Banana
Google

A fast image model I use for quick visual sketches when I want to see what a concept could look like before I commit to a composition. Exploratory, not final.

Used for  reference imagery, mood exploration
Claude Design
Anthropic

Generates layout variants and component concepts I can react to. I use it as a way to see options faster than I could sketch them myself.

Used for  page concepting, layout options

UI generation from a brief. Produces mocks that I pull apart for ideas rather than use wholesale the useful output is usually a detail, not the whole screen.

03.3  ·  Per-assignment breakdown

What I did  /  what the models did

For each piece on this site, here is the split. Nothing hidden, nothing dressed up.

Research Trends Class Assignment  ·  Longform scroll

What I did

  • Chose the research question and the five-year window.
  • Read the annotated bibliography and picked which threads were worth tracing.
  • Wrote every claim about the meaning of the shifts.
  • Designed the scroll pacing and the tone.
  • Made the final call on which vocabulary changes actually matter.

What the tools did

  • NotebookLM  surfaced terminology trends across the source set.
  • Claude  drafted HTML/CSS and iterated on copy when asked.
  • Grammarly  tightened prose at line level.
  • Claude Design / Stitch  offered layout variants I pulled from.
Reading Response Class Assignment  ·  Week 3

What I did

  • Read the assigned text carefully; no AI summary substitute.
  • Chose my three themes and the angle on each.
  • Wrote the position I was willing to defend in class.
  • Made editorial calls on what to cut for pace.

What the tools did

  • Claude  pushed back on weak points in early drafts.
  • Grammarly  caught sentence-level awkwardness.
  • Claude Design  suggested the theme-tile visual structure.
Personal Project Independent work  ·  In progress

What I did

  • Picked the topic and what the project is actually trying to do.
  • Am still working out the shape of the argument.
  • Deliberately held the page back rather than posting a clean placeholder that would misrepresent where I am.

What the tools did

  • Claude  is helping me stress-test framings in conversation.
  • No AI-generated abstract is on the page, by choice, until I have written one I stand behind.
The site itself Infrastructure  ·  Design system

What I did

  • Specified the aesthetic (Scientific Sublime) and the editorial stance.
  • Decided the information architecture and URL structure.
  • Reviewed every page at multiple viewport sizes and flagged regressions.
  • Own the GitHub repo and the deploys.

What the tools did

  • Claude  and Antigravity wrote most of the HTML and CSS.
  • Claude Design / Stitch  generated the first-pass layout ideas.
  • Nano Banana  produced exploratory imagery I did not end up using.
03.4  ·  What I still do

The parts I will not delegate

Three things on this site are mine, even when a tool helped produce the sentences around them.

The position. Whether the reading response takes side A or side B is my call. I do not ask a model for its opinion and then inherit it; I decide what I think and then use models to test whether my reasoning holds up.

The editorial voice. If a draft sounds like a chatbot it does not ship. I reject smoothed-over, explanatory, middle-of-the-road AI prose. The voice on these pages has opinions and a rhythm, and those are mine.

The sources. I do not let models invent citations. If a claim is sourced, I have read the source. NotebookLM is useful precisely because it can only speak from the documents I give it and cannot invent anything beyond them.

There is also a fourth, harder one: knowing when to stop. AI tools make it easy to keep iterating forever. A deadline, and a willingness to publish something a little less than perfect, is a human decision I have tried to protect.

03.5  ·  Closing

A note to anyone reading as a grader

If you are reading this in an evaluative capacity, the honest summary is: I used AI extensively, and I would have produced worse work without it. That is not a brag it is the same thing a student in an earlier decade might have said about a library with open stacks, or a spell-checker, or a laptop.

The interesting question, for this course in particular, is not whether using these tools is acceptable, but whether the way I used them left me with more understanding or less. My claim is the former. I read more sources because NotebookLM made the review tractable. I wrote more cleanly because Grammarly held up a mirror. I designed better pages because Claude let me see five layouts before committing to one. And I thought more carefully about my positions because I had a partner available to push back on them at any hour.

The site is a record of that. Every piece here went through AI hands; none of it, I hope, reads like AI wrote it.

The goal was never to hide the machinery. The goal was to make sure the judgment in the work was still mine.
