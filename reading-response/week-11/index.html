Control learned to be convenient.

Three readings on technofascism. Gonzalez shows the visible edge, the raid and the facial recognition contract. Coeckelbergh, the main reading, argues the real danger is quieter, a matter of platforms and defaults rather than jackboots. Moellers says stop watching Silicon Valley and look at the ordinary machinery of the state. I study a program that hands every Japanese child a Google account, so I read all three with one uneasy question. When control arrives as a convenience, would I recognise it in my own field?

01  ·  The visible edge

First, the part you can photograph.

Gonzalez is the short, sharp one, an opinion editorial written from fifteen miles away as ICE raids turned violent in his own city. He follows the money and the hardware. Venture firms fund the startups, Clearview scrapes billions of faces, Palantir builds the deportation platform, and Flock's cameras read every passing plate. This is the technofascism you can point a camera at. It has a helmet, a database, and a contract number.

The line that stays with me is his last move, that the tools built for the border are the ones most ready to be turned inward on the people the state decides to call enemies. He does not want scholars to keep watching politely. He wants them to choose a side.

These companies’ products are likely to be unleashed not only for foreign wars, but against people residing in the US who Trump refers to as “the enemy within.” Gonzalez 2026, p. 6
02  ·  The quieter mechanism

Then the part you cannot.

Coeckelbergh is the main reading, and he moves the danger out of sight. Classical fascism marched and shouted. Western technofascism, he argues, prefers convenience. It works through data extraction, algorithmic governance, behavioural nudging, and platform monopolisation, all of it sold as personalisation and ease. Nobody has to be dragged anywhere. You are simply profiled, defaulted, and gently steered, and you rarely feel the hand on your back.

He builds this from the classic theory, Arendt and the Frankfurt School, Paxton and Eco, not to replace them but as a toolbox for the present. The idea I keep circling is the banality of evil, updated for the machine. It is the moment a caseworker defers to a confident automated recommendation and stops asking whether it is right, because the system feels objective and the queue is long.

Technofascism in the West tends to operate through quieter, more insidious mechanisms of control, such as data extraction, algorithmic governance, behavioral nudging, and platform monopolization. Coeckelbergh 2026
03  ·  The alliance

Efficiency is the whole pitch.

Two of Coeckelbergh's mechanisms point straight at my field. The first is the corporatist alliance. Big Tech, he notes, is not really a free market outcome at all. It grows through contracts with the state, and that entanglement mirrors the corporate and state alliances that were a hallmark of the historical regimes. The second is the cult of efficiency, the celebration of technological power over humanistic values, empathy quietly recast as a weakness that slows progress down.

Hold those two side by side and you have described a great deal of how education technology gets sold to a ministry. A public good, delivered by a private platform, justified almost entirely by how much faster and smoother it makes the work.

The close entanglement of Big Tech firms with state institutions mirrors the corporate-state alliances that were hallmarks of fascist regimes. Coeckelbergh 2026
04  ·  The cloud in the classroom

My field runs on the same cloud.

This is where the reading stops being about somebody else. GIGA School is a national program that put a device into the hands of every schoolchild in Japan, and for a large share of them the account behind that device belongs to Google. A public ministry, a private cloud, one login per child. That is Coeckelbergh's corporatist alliance almost to the letter, except it is wearing a school uniform and nobody in the room is afraid of it. The efficiency pitch was the reason it was funded at all.

I want to be careful here, because I have spent my fieldwork watching what teachers actually do with these tools, and it is often genuinely good. The same platform that logs a child's every document is the one that lets a teacher give a drifting child a reason to stay in the lesson. The lever cuts both ways. What Coeckelbergh hands me is not a verdict on GIGA. It is a vocabulary for the part of it I had let myself call convenient. Data extraction. Platform monopolisation. Defaults that decide for you. I reach for those tools every week and I had never once thought to call them by these names.

The only thing standing between the default and the child is what the teacher knows well enough to override, which my field calls TPACK. And my thesis already suspects that this knowledge is unevenly held and mostly picked up by chance.

05  ·  The ordinary machine

Stop looking for the villain.

Moellers is the sharpest of the three, and she turns the camera around. Fixating on Silicon Valley, she argues, is a distraction. What actually enables technofascism is ordinary, the normal operations of the legitimate institutions of the modern state, its offices and borders and schools and forms. Drawing on Toscano and the black radical tradition, she reminds us that for colonised and racialised people the fascist condition was never new and never needed a smartphone.

I read that and felt how Western the whole conversation had been, Gonzalez and Coeckelbergh both writing from and about the American and European scene. My own work is comparative, Japan and Thailand, and Moellers is the one who tells me why that comparison matters. If technofascism lives in the ordinary state and not only in California, then a Japanese classroom and a Bangkok pilot school are not outside the story. They are exactly the kind of ordinary infrastructure she says to watch. The GIGA login is not a dramatic villain. It is a normal Tuesday, and that is the entire point.

Our struggle is not Silicon Valley, it is global racial capitalism supercharged by the digital infrastructures of state violence. Moellers 2025, p. 254
06  ·  Where it leaves me

I do not get to feel safe about it.

Read in the order I was given, the three pieces hand you down a staircase. Gonzalez shows the visible edge, the raid you can photograph. Coeckelbergh shows the covert mechanism, the control that arrives as convenience. Moellers shows the address where it actually lives, the ordinary machinery of the state. The GIGA classroom sits on that bottom step, and I did not put it there. The readings did.

I am not ready to call GIGA technofascist, and I do not think any of these authors would hand me that sentence for free. The device is still the thing a good teacher uses to reach a child who was drifting. But I can no longer file the infrastructure under convenient and look away. The most I can honestly hold is the tension. The same cloud that helps a teacher design a good lesson is a corporatist alliance she did not choose and cannot leave, and the only margin of freedom in the room is whatever she knows well enough to override.

So the question I am left with is not whether the technology is good or bad. It is quieter than that, and it is pointed at me. If control has learned to arrive as a gift, and if it lives in exactly the ordinary places I study, then how would I know it when I am the one handing it out?
