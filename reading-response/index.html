Does it matter who teaches anymore?

A meta-synthesis of 23 studies asks how elementary students experience AI in the classroom. The answer keeps pointing to the same variable, and it isn't the device.

Scroll to begin
01  ·  The synthesis

23 studies. 10 countries. One pattern.

The article synthesises qualitative research on elementary students' perspectives on AI; ages 6–12, across China, South Korea, Turkey, the US, New Zealand, Sweden, Nigeria and others. Four themes hold the synthesis together.

Theme 01
Role of AI in teaching
Autonomy, personalised learning, motivation, and a sense of socialisation; students experience AI as something that changes the shape of the classroom, not just the content.
Theme 02
Students' AI perceptions
Cognitive (robot or tool → learning system), affective (curiosity and fear), and conceptual (ethics, authority, who gets to decide what the system says).
Theme 03
How students use AI
Entertainment, ease of use, prompt difficulties, and security and ethics concerns that children raise without being prompted.
Theme 04
Role in the education system
Equality of opportunity, integration barriers, and the pedagogical issues that surface when commercial tools meet under-designed learning environments.
The pedagogically sound use of AI tools in primary education and effective guidance by teachers play an important role in supporting children's digital learning experiences. Polat & Sarıdaş 2026
02  ·  What struck me

The device was never the point.

Two things stood out.

First: students who haven't used AI describe it as a robot or a vacuum cleaner. Students who have used it describe it as a friend, a tutor, a source of anxiety about control. The gap isn't about intelligence but rather about how they interact.

Second: upper elementary students (grades 5–6) independently raise data privacy and cheating concerns without being taught to. The ethical instinct is already there. It's just that the curriculum have not caught up to it yet.

03  ·  My reading

Same gap, different scale.

Reading across the article's four themes, I kept thinking about the trend in ICT implementation in the educational context that the factor that determines whether EdTech (including AI) deepens learning or merely entertains is how the learning environment was designed by the teacher, not device access (it's one of the aspects, but not all). This leads to exactly what I find in Japan's GIGA School Program and Thailand's digital classroom initiatives: both invested heavily in hardware deployment, and both ran into the same wall when it came to pedagogical transformation. The article gives the student-level empirical evidence that the policy literature often lacks.

What I find more complicated, and not yet resolved, is the article's optimism around equality of opportunity. The authors argue that AI's non-judgmental quality promotes greater participation among students who might otherwise disengage, and the evidence supports this. But the article also shows that students in low-access contexts have fundamentally different AI perceptions and outcomes than those in high-access settings. AI may provide an equality within a well-resourced classroom while simultaneously widening the gap between schools that do not have those capacities. GIGA achieved near-universal device access at the national level; even so, part of the evidence in the article suggests that it still isn't enough and this is a more serious case for Thailand, where the devices and infrastructure are not even deployed universally.

04  ·  What we still don't know / What should I study next?

Who is setting the pedagogical agenda?

The synthesis covers ages 10–12 in 18 of 23 studies. Almost nothing on younger children. More importantly: every study cited runs on commercial platforms such as ChatGPT, Google Gemini, Siri. There is no education-specific AI in the sample.

If schooling's relationship with AI is mediated entirely through general-purpose commercial tools, the question of who is actually designing the learning experience becomes urgent. Especially when students are more exposed to those AI tools, the learning environment should be able to support the use and make an example of how they could be AI literate. Moreover, the field of how teachers develop their instructional design skills in the setting of ICT-integrated classrooms, which is what I'm trying to study in my master's thesis, will become more urgent.

The missing variable The article asks how students experience AI. The next question: how do teachers design around commercial tools they didn't build and can't change?
