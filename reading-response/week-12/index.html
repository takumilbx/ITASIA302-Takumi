The frontier was never empty.

One reading this week, Hughes on what he calls techno-colonialism, four figures who decided democracy was hopeless and tried to build floating cities on the ocean instead. Some of the same names came up in last week's technofascism readings so this felt like a prequel to that.

01  ·  After reading

After I read it.

The short version: Land, Yarvin, Thiel, and Friedman gave up on democracy and tried to build floating city-states on the ocean, because the ocean looked like the last empty place on earth. It was not, legally UNCLOS regulates basically all of it, and when the plan met reality they ended up negotiating with French Polynesia, a former colony threatened by rising seas, which is the opposite of finding land with nobody on it.

What I felt after finishing was mostly discomfort about one word, empty. The whole project runs on calling a place empty, and Hughes spends the paper showing that empty was never a fact about the ocean, it was just the word that made settling it feel harmless.

We need a new frontier – a blank canvas on which social or constitutional entrepreneurs can create their products and test them in reality by seeing if they can attract citizens. Friedman and Taylor, quoted in Hughes 2026, p. 83
02  ·  My context

It connects to my field.

It connects to my context of GIGA School and the Bangkok pilot schools, because ed-tech policy talks the same way, pilot schools, sandboxes, rollouts, as if the classroom were empty ground where a new system just lands and works the way it did in the presentation slides. But teachers are already there, with their own practice and judgment about when a tool helps and when it gets in the way, which is the knowledge my field calls TPACK and my thesis is about where it comes from.

The French Polynesia part also sits uncomfortably close to Bangkok, one of the fastest sinking capitals in the world, whose pilot schools borrow models and platforms from richer systems, including Japan's program and Google's cloud, which is exactly the borrowing I study. I do not think it is the same thing, Hughes himself warns against calling every experiment settler colonialism and French Polynesia had the power to say no and used it, but the similarity bothered me more than I expected.

To this extent, there is an attempt to exploit an asymmetry of power within the international state system. Hughes 2026, p. 85
03  ·  Still asking

What I still ask.

I am still left with the same question at two scales. When a vulnerable system borrows technology, who sets the defaults and who can realistically say no. And for myself, when I go into a Bangkok classroom with my interview guide, whether I am mapping what is already there or acting like it is empty.
