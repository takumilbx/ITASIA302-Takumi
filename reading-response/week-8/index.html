Boredom is a failure of expectation.

Daniel Jütte goes looking for boredom in the first place I'd would expect to find it (I was in Buddhist school so kinda relate)., where people kept falling asleep during the sermon. What he finds is that this was never a sign of weak faith. It was a sign that the service did not fit the people sitting in it. This is a response about what that means for a classroom full of tablets.

01  ·  The claim that landed

The congregation was not the problem.

What resonates with me most about Jütte’s article is how he reframes what boredom actually means. Rather than treating it as a personal failing or a sign of laziness, he argues that boredom comes from situations that are predictable and very hard to escape. He borrows a sociological definition and calls it a “failure of expectation.” He writes that church must have felt to many people like “an elaborate and long-winded ceremony that left little room for individual participation.”

The part I found interesting is that he does not read this as a loss of faith. The people falling asleep in the pews “were not opposed to God’s word.” Their inability to stay focused instead pointed to “significant shortcomings in the way that scripture was taught and expounded in church.” So it implies that it is a structural problem and it simply did not fit the people it was supposed to serve.

Those who fell asleep or were otherwise inattentive in church were not opposed to God’s word; instead, their inability to stay focused suggests significant shortcomings in the way that scripture was taught and expounded in church. Jütte 2020  ·  p. 1163
02  ·  The same logic, a different room

Different students take it in differently.

Reading that, I immediately thought about active learning. If a lesson is too rigid and leaves no room for students to take part, of course they lose focus. They are not lazy. The design just does not fit them, and I feel this is the same thing Jütte is describing, only in a classroom (which is arguably also boring lol).

When a lesson is built in a one-size-fits-all way, the students who learn differently are the ones who check out first. Not because they do not care, but because the design failed them. Different students take in information differently. Some do better with reading, some with visual materials, some by doing and moving. I do not see those differences as a problem, but I see them as something a teacher needs to understand and plan around.

03  ·  The more expensive pew

Technology was supposed to fix engagement.

This connects directly to what I see in GIGA School classrooms. When 1:1 devices were introduced in Japan, there was an assumption that the technology itself would fix the engagement problem and realize "active learning". But I think giving a student a tablet in a classroom where the teacher still lectures the whole time is not very different from what Jütte describes about the pews.

He explains that the Protestant Reformation put long sermons at the centre of the service, and that pews became common in churches around the same time. They were added to make the long sermons easier to sit through, and they ended up making it easier to fall asleep. The pew did not cause the boredom, but it absorbed it. It made an unengaging structure bearable, so there was less pressure to fix the structure at all. I think a digital device in education context can do the same thing.

If shorter sermons were out of the question, other concessions had to be made; it is no coincidence that pews and benches became a common feature of Protestant churches in this context. Jütte 2020  ·  p. 1166

I saw a version of this in my own fieldwork. A primary school science teacher I interviewed told me that when the tablets first arrived, the mood was to “use tablets for everything,” and that “a lot of it didn’t work well.” Looking back, I think the problem was that the device was being added on top of the lesson instead of changing how the lesson worked. She has spent the years since then figuring out where the device actually helps and where it does not.

04  ·  Where the work actually is

The work is not the device.

The potential is real, because a device can support multimodal learning in ways a chalkboard cannot. Students can read, watch, listen, and record all in one place. But that potential only becomes real when the teacher knows how to design tasks that fit the range of learners in the room. Without that, the screen just turns the lecture into a slightly more expensive version of itself.

The same teacher gave me a small but clear example. When her students record experiment data, she assigns one role per group, because if she does not, some students have nothing to do and get bored. The recording job is plain, but as she put it, once “you get to touch the tablet” it becomes something students want to do. She is not using the device to deliver content. She is using it to make sure everyone has a reason to stay in the activity.

What I find important is where that knowledge comes from. When I asked her how she learned to teach this way, she estimated that around ninety percent of it came from colleagues and from hes own practice, and only about ten percent from formal training. She told me she does not use anything she learned in her university courses. So the knowledge that turns a device into a tool instead of a pew is real, but it is mostly picked up informally, and that makes it uneven and hard to guarantee.

05  ·  What this leaves me with

Are the devices becoming a more expensive pew?

Jütte ends with a question that I keep thinking about. “But is it always our attention that is deficient?” he asks. “Or does the deficiency lie in things that fail to capture our attention?” He is writing about sermons, but it lands directly on the screen in front of me. We are quick to say students cannot focus, and slow to ask whether the thing in front of them was worth focusing on in the first place.

That is the question I keep coming back to in my own research. I look at how teachers actually build the knowledge to teach well with these devices, and whether the system around them helps them build it. So I am left with one simple question. Are teachers in 1:1 device classrooms learning to use these devices in a way that deepens attention, or are the devices just becoming a more expensive pew?

But is it always our attention that is deficient? Or does the deficiency lie in things that fail to capture our attention? Jütte 2020  ·  p. 1174
