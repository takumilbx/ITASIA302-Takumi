Who gets to write the history of AI?

Six historians argue that every origin story AI tells about itself is a deliberate choice. Main narratives such as Dartmouth (1956) and Turing (1950) are clean, Western, and positioned as academic. This paper asks what got left out from the main narratives and why that matters more than the main narratives itself.

01  ·  The argument

One technology. Centuries of history.

The paper introduces a special issue of BJHS (British Journal for the History of Science) Themes collecting 18 articles from historians around the world. Four themes emerge from the collection of articles.

Theme 01
Hidden Labour
The AI model were being built somewhere in the world, where someone is reviewing images of violence for $1.50 an hour to make that possible. This theme argues that AI does not remove human work, instead it buries it.
Theme 02
Encoded Behaviour
The logic AI uses to sort and classify people did not appear in 1956. It was already there in eugenics, colonial census-taking, carceral record-keeping. The technology is new, while the logic behind it is not.
Theme 03
Disingenuous Rhetoric
The authors argue that AI’s self-narration (Turing, Dartmouth, progress) is not descriptive but constitutive. The story selects what AI is allowed to be and forecloses what it is not.
Theme 04
Cognitive Injustice
Whose definition of intelligence gets built into the system? The authors argue the question is not rhetorical. The answer determines which populations are legible to AI and which are made into edge cases.
Rather than fixate on the latest promissory technology… we explore AI as a diffuse set of technologies and systems of epistemic and political power. Ali et al. 2023
02  ·  What struck me

The framework was never neutral.

Cognitive injustice was the idea that stayed with me. Not because it is the most dramatic claim in the paper, but because it quietly explained something I had already encountered without knowing what to call it.

For my thesis I use TPACK, a framework for understanding how teachers develop knowledge about technology. It was built at Michigan State. When researchers in Japan tried to apply it to their classrooms, things did not fit. The original categories did not map cleanly onto how Japanese teachers actually worked, so they spent years restructuring the model, merging two of its components, rebuilding parts of it from scratch. All of that, just to use a tool that was supposed to be universal.

I had read that story before and filed it under “localisation.” Ali et al. reframe it. It is not localisation. It is the cost you pay for using a framework built somewhere specific, for someone specific, that was never quite told its own assumptions were assumptions. Honestly, it makes me question realities I had taken as given, and I am still not sure what to do with that.

03  ·  What I still don’t know

What does a framework that can’t see you look like?

The paper argues that every framework for intelligence encodes a definition of what counts as knowing. Once that definition is built in, it determines which populations are legible and which become edge cases. Ali et al. are writing about AI systems, but the logic applies directly to TPACK.

The categories assume that a teacher’s relationship to technology is primarily a question of knowledge. What they know about content. What they know about pedagogy. What they know about the tools. But knowledge is not the only thing shaping what a teacher does in a classroom. Institutional structure, peer dynamics, what counts as appropriate deference to a supervisor in a specific context. None of that fits inside TK, PK, or CK.

Ali et al. would say the omission is not accidental. It reflects what the researchers who built the framework thought counted as a variable in the first place. That is the part I do not yet know how to answer in my own work, and I am hoping to explore that question in my master’s thesis.

The missing question Not just “how do teachers acquire TPACK?” But “what does TPACK miss about how teachers in these contexts think about knowledge in the first place?”
