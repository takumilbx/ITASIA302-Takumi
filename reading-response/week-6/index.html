Technology as amplifier. But of what?

The Friends of Attention argue that school exists not to transmit information but to form attention: to teach us what is worth noticing and how long to stay with it. This is a response about what happens when the technology we bring into schools amplifies the wrong thing.

Scroll to begin
01  ·  The claim that landed

School was never really about knowledge.

When I first read the claim that “people go to school to learn how to attend” (not to acquire facts, not to gain a trade, but to learn how to give attention, and to what), my instinct was to push back. But the more I thought about it, the more I recognised something true in it. I think school, at its best, has always been more about forming the student than filling her. What kinds of things deserve concentration? How do you stay with something long enough for it to mean something? Chemistry class trains you to attend to data, patterns, and evidence. History class trains you to attend to time, cause, and perspective. The subject is almost secondary.

My own experience of school felt something like this. I feel I was taught not just to receive information but to act upon it, to question what is worth considering and to weigh what kind of knowledge deserves sustained attention. That distinction mattered to me. But I also think this is not the experience most schools deliver. Many schools operate closer to a different model: give out information, test recall, move on. And I think this problem has gotten worse, not better, with the arrival of digital tools.

What I find useful about the book is that it gives this observation a historical structure. This is a recurring failure, not a new one.

02  ·  The Industrial Revolution is still running

The classroom as quasi-factory of mind-numbingly rote tasks.

The book is careful to say that attentional formation is not inherently liberatory. “As often as not,” the authors write, “schools have served to acculturate new generations to kinds of attention that consign them to lifeways of cramped spirits and even outright exploitation.” I think the Industrial Revolution example is the clearest one they give: students in Birmingham were learning to attend, but to forms of attention prefabricated for factory labour. The ways of seeing and doing they acquired were, as the book puts it, “mostly prefabricated for the exercise of dull and demeaning labour.”

Study is what you do with other people. It’s talking and walking around with other people, working, dancing, suffering, some irreducible convergence of all three. Fred Moten, cited in Attensity! p. 135-136

What the book offers as the counter is an expanded definition of school: jazz bands, tai chi in the park, chess clubs, fishing at the pier, Fred Moten’s study groups. I find this genuinely compelling. These are communities of attention and they form you differently, toward understanding rather than toward compliance, toward the world rather than toward the metric.

03  ·  Who has access to the jazz band?

The informal community is not equally available.

I think the book’s expanded definition of school is genuinely useful, but it also makes me uneasy. The informal communities the authors name (chess clubs, surf groups, tai chi in the park) are not equally available to everyone. I feel they assume a certain kind of neighbourhood, a certain kind of time, a certain kind of social infrastructure. For students in under-resourced communities, those alternatives are precisely what has been stripped away, and not gradually. It happens as a direct consequence of the economic pressures that under-resourced communities absorb the most.

This matters because the authors are arguing that these informal communities of attention are the antidote to the attention economy. But if you cannot access them, you are left with only the formal school. And if that school is increasingly organised around digital platforms optimising for completion metrics (lessons finished, badges earned, time-on-task recorded), I think the cost is borne entirely by the student who is least equipped to absorb it.

I feel the book is stronger as a critique than as an equity argument. It names the problem of attentional monoculture clearly. But it is quieter about who tends to be left in that monoculture with no exit.

04  ·  Not one gap. Two.

The platform does not arrive neutrally.

When I think about how digital learning platforms actually land in schools, I see two layers of distortion, not one.

The first is the teacher layer. A well-resourced classroom has a teacher who uses the platform as one tool among many, corrected for when it falls short, supplemented when it is not enough. An under-resourced classroom may have the platform as the teacher. The educator who does exist is overloaded: not with fewer tasks, but with more. I think the platform generates data, tracking demands, and reporting requirements that bury her. She cannot correct for the platform’s limits because she is already submerged under its outputs.

The second is the student layer. A student in a well-resourced school learns to use the platform strategically, to complete and then go beyond. A student in a platform-dependent classroom learns to give attention to completion itself: the lesson finished, the badge earned, the metric satisfied. I feel this is attention trained toward an endpoint that serves the application’s corporate interest, not her own understanding.

There is no way to outsource understanding. And there is no other path to freedom. Burnett et al., Attensity! p. 135

I think of the platform, in both cases, as an amplifier. In the first classroom, it amplifies human teaching. In the second, it amplifies the distortion. I think the more honest question is not whether to use it, but what exactly it is amplifying, and for whom.

05  ·  What this leaves me with

Policy cannot just be about access.

My research examines digital learning materials policy: how institutions decide what tools go into classrooms, and whether those choices serve quality education for all students. I think this reading sharpens a question I have been circling for a while.

Policy conversations about digital learning tend to centre on access: who has a device, who has connectivity, who has the software licence. I feel the Attensity! argument forces a prior question: what is the technology amplifying, and for whom? A policy that delivers a completion-optimised platform equally to every classroom has not achieved equity. I think it has delivered the Industrial Revolution classroom at scale, with a login screen.

What the book leaves me with is a criterion I did not have before. I wonder if digital learning materials should be evaluated not only by what they teach, but by what kind of attention they form. Do they amplify human study, the kind that happens between people, irreducibly intellectual and alive? Or do they replace it with something that looks like learning and measures like completion? I do not yet know what a policy framework built on that criterion would look like. But reading this has made me think that the current ones are asking the wrong question.
