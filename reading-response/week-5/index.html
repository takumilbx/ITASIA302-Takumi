Fluent systems, buried questions.

Selsbæk-Reitz introduces the concept of promptism,the uncritical belief that a well-phrased question (prompts) to an LLM will yield a reliable, objective answer. This is a response about why fluency can be the trap, not the feature.

01  ·  The familiar slogan

No bad outputs, only bad prompts?

When I first read Selsbæk-Reitz’s definition of promptism, which is the uncritical belief that a well-phrased question (prompts) to an AI will yield a reliable, objective answer, my mind went immediately to a slogan I have heard repeated online for years: "there are no bad AI outputs, only bad prompts".

In my opinion, there is a partial truth in it. Better prompting does extract more capability from the model. However, the slogan quietly mixes up three different things, which are capability, reliability, and objectivity. A perfectly engineered prompt can return a fluent, confident answer that is wrong or worse, an answer that is “true” only from one perspective baked deep into the training data, while quietly silencing others.

Reading this sharpened something I had been circling since the introduction of LLMs. Even when I know to ask truth according to whom?, I often cannot follow the question all the way through. The AI does not show me where its answer came from. The reasoning chain is gone. There is no source to interrogate, no author to question. I am left to verify against something, I do not know what it is.

02  ·  Why fluency is the trap

The Smoothness Problem.

Selsbæk-Reitz names it the Smoothness Problem. Fluency bypasses critical instinct before content is even evaluated. We are wired to trust ease. When a sentence arrives polished, structured, and confidently delivered, we relax. I think the trap is not that AI lies to us. The trap is that AI sounds like it cannot be lying.

The quiet collapse of interpretation into convenience. Selsbæk-Reitz 2026

The word that stays with me from his definition is convenience, not efficiency. Efficiency implies you are still optimizing toward a goal. Convenience implies you have stopped caring enough to optimize. The danger of generative AI is not that it makes us faster. It is that it makes us comfortable not thinking. And it does so without ever announcing itself as a problem, the surface is too smooth for that.

03  ·  The turn toward literacy

Not better tools, better readers.

Part III is where the book stops diagnosing and starts proposing. Two chapters stand out: Chapter 8 — Teaching People to Read Machines, and Chapter 12 — You Are Not a Prompt. Together, they frame the central response that what we need is not better tools, not better prompts, but better readers.

Chapter 8 argues that what we currently call digital literacy is outdated. It was built for static objects such as articles, videos, posts, etc. These are things that have authors and traceable lineages. Generative AI does not produce those. It produces outputs: language assembled in real time by a system optimized for responsiveness, not truth. Some LLMs now can show their sources, but there are still cases when the sources are wrong or misleading. So being able to check the sources does not mean that the outputs are reliable. The literacy required now is not about spotting fake URLs. It is about reading the system itself and recognizing when a model is predicting rather than reasoning, noticing the absence of the things that used to anchor a claim.

Chapter 12 names what is at stake. The danger is not that AI will deceive us. The danger is that AI will quietly persuade us that no further thinking is required. It is the surrender of interpretive agency.

The principle AI should be a starting point, never an endpoint. The answer we receive is not a conclusion. It is a proposal for us to verify, compare, and interpret.
04  ·  Where this lands hardest

Students first, educators unprepared.

If promptism is a structural problem rooted in cognition and design, then the people most vulnerable to it are not experts but students (people encountering topics for the first time, without the prior knowledge needed to detect when an output is plausible but wrong). And the people who should be helping them build that resistance, like educators, are not often literate in these tools yet. Not because they are not capable, but because the literacy required is genuinely new and genuinely hard (even for experts!). It is not just technical but philosophical, what does it mean to “know,” and what does it mean when a system that does not know speaks as if it does?

The problem is, as seen in Week 3 and the school (even universities) curriculum, we have spent decades failing to meaningfully integrate media and information literacy into curricula. Now we are being asked to integrate something philosophically and technically harder, and we are being asked to do it urgently. If we do not, the consequences are serious. Dominant knowledge gets encoded into the model. It is then reproduced fluently and convincingly to the next generation of learners. The cycle of structural bias becomes harder to interrupt rather than easier.

The antidote author proposes is to cultivate curiosity about how the tool works, what it cannot do, where its data comes from, and critical thinking, the discipline of refusing easy answers, of treating an answer as a proposal rather than a conclusion. These are not building new habits for AI literacy. But they have to be deliberately taught now, because the environment no longer rewards them by default (even AI systems nudge us to be lazy!).

05  ·  What this leaves me with

TPACK meets its interlocutor.

My own thesis examines how teachers develop TPACK (Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge) in one-to-one device environments. TPACK as a framework was theorized around tools: teachers learning what a tablet can do, how a digital resource integrates with a curriculum. Selsbæk-Reitz’s argument suggests something the original framework may not fully capture. Generative AI does not behave like a tool. It behaves like an interlocutor. And the knowledge teachers need in order to integrate it pedagogically may be different from the technological knowledge TPACK was originally designed to describe.

I do not yet know what to do with that observation. But reading this part of a book have made me think about how urgent it is for us to equip ourselves with AI literacy, let alone students. This is because the danger of promptism, the comfortable not thinking, is everywhere nowadays, and it is something that need to be addressed before the most vulnerable among us, the students, are being harmed more than they already are in their daily life's exporse to Generative AI tools.
