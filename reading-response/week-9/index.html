The room changed before the children did.

Matthew Smith finds ADHD not in the brain but in classrooms, in Cold War politics, and in a drug that arrived before the diagnosis did. I spend my days watching teachers build classrooms on purpose, so I really resonate with it, and it left me with one question. Who do we decide to blame when a room does not fit the child sitting inside it?

01  ·  The claim that landed

ADHD was built, not found.

What comes to me first is that ADHD was built, not discovered. The fidgeting and the impulsiveness and the wandering attention were, for most of the early twentieth century, just behaviours a teacher might find annoying, not symptoms, and the children who worried doctors then were the opposite kind, the withdrawn and the nervous ones. Then around 1957 the worry flipped, and what convinces me is that nothing had changed inside the children. What changed, and changed fast, was the world that had decided to start measuring them.

When the origins of ADHD are examined, it becomes clear that a wide range of social, educational, political, and technological factors have contributed to making behaviours once seen as fairly normal transform into the most commonly diagnosed childhood psychiatric disorder of the modern era. Smith 2014
02  ·  The room changed

The disorder arrived with the desk.

The part that lands hardest is that the disorder arrived with the desk, not the child. Progressive classrooms let children move, build, and do, so a restless child was no problem there, because the room never asked anyone to sit still. After Sputnik the room changed. Desks went into a grid facing the front, and the same child who used to thrive was suddenly the one who could not cope and was easy to single out. The child stayed the same. The room is what turned.

The new, more subject-centred classrooms required students to remain seated in their desks, which were arranged in a grid pattern, facing the teacher. Not only did this prevent active children from gaining the stimuli that helped to keep them focused, teachers could easily identify those who fidgeted, got out of their seats, bothered other children, day-dreamed, or otherwise acted out. Smith 2014
03  ·  A room built on purpose

I watch a teacher design attention.

Here the reading stops being history and starts describing my own field. This spring I spent two visits with a science teacher in a GIGA School classroom in Tokyo, a teacher I will call T-sensei, and what I kept noting was how deliberately she decided, lesson by lesson, when work stayed on paper and when it moved to the screen. She designs the room on purpose, and she can take the design apart again whenever a lesson needs it.

When her students record experiment data she gives one student per group the job of typing it in, because otherwise a few of them drift off with nothing to do. The job is plain, but the moment you “get to touch the tablet” it becomes something a child wants to do, so she is using the device less to deliver content than to give every child a reason to stay in the activity. The same lever Smith describes is sitting in her room. The design decides who counts as attentive, and the only difference is that she works it on purpose.

04  ·  Who gets caught

It is easier to fix the child.

What stays with me is who pays when a room does not fit. The children labelled and medicated were the disadvantaged ones, and once Ritalin made the whole thing feel like medicine rather than discipline, everyone around the child could move the problem somewhere safer to look at.

By providing a neurochemical solution, it also suggested that the root of the problem was not poor teaching or parenting, but neurological dysfunction. Smith 2014

That is the sentence I keep going back to, because it names the cheap move exactly. When a room fails a child it is always easier to find the fault in the child than in the design, and that is as true in a 2026 classroom full of tablets as in a grid of 1950s desks.

This is where my own research makes me uneasy. Whether a room fits is mostly a function of what the teacher knows, what my field calls TPACK, and yet T-sensei guessed that around ninety percent of her skill came from colleagues and her own trial and error, and only ten percent from formal training. She does not really use what she was taught at university. If that skill is real but unevenly held and picked up by chance, then some rooms will keep failing some children, and we already know what we do then. We call it the child’s problem.

05  ·  The black box

A category built somewhere specific.

Smith calls ADHD a black box, a concept used so widely that almost nobody asks any more how it was built or what got sealed inside it. I have run into this shape before. A few weeks ago I was writing about cognitive injustice, the price a field pays when it treats a framework built in one place, for one kind of person, as if it were true everywhere. TPACK, the framework my thesis leans on, was built at Michigan State, and when Japanese researchers tried to use it, it did not fit, so they had to rebuild it. ADHD is that same story on a larger stage, assembled out of one American moment and now exported to places like China and India as though it had always been simply true.

I want to be careful the way Smith is, because he is not saying that no child struggles. Many children do, for reasons that run from stress to poor sleep, so the point was never to deny it. The point is to stop reaching for the simplest label and to do the slower, harder work for the particular child in front of you. So I am left holding the box open. Before I let myself decide that a child cannot pay attention, I would rather ask what room we built, who it was really built for, and whether it was ever built with that child in mind at all.

We should also think about why people, in differing cultures and contexts, decide to embrace or reject biomedical notions of ADHD and the pharmaceutical treatments provided for the disorder. Smith 2014
